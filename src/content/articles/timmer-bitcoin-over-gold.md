Even the assets people buy to escape the stock market have been selling off with it. Gold and bitcoin both slid in the latest risk-off move. Still, Jurrien Timmer, Fidelity's director of global macro, says he would rather hold bitcoin than gold for now.
What Timmer said
In a post on X on Nov. 7, Timmer said he still prefers bitcoin to gold for the time being. His reason is that bitcoin's price relative to gold is holding up, even though both have fallen.
An earlier post gave the context. Timmer said gold and bitcoin have become more closely tied to stocks and have "joined the risk-off party" in recent weeks. He put their correlation with the S&P 500 at 60% to 70%, according to the reports we read of his posts.
Correlation measures how closely two things move together. A reading of 100% means they move in lockstep, and 0% means no link at all. People often buy gold and bitcoin as a hedge against stock-market trouble. At a 60% to 70% reading, they have been hedging much less than usual.
The numbers behind the week
Timmer's comments came at the end of a rough week for risk assets, and Friday's jobs report added to the pressure.
The U.S. economy added 165,000 jobs in October, far above forecasts of roughly 59,000 to 65,000. The unemployment rate edged down to 4.1% from 4.2%, and the August and September figures were revised up by a combined 84,000 jobs, according to CNBC and Reuters.

Strong jobs data makes it more likely the Fed keeps tightening. According to CME FedWatch, as reported by Fox Business, traders put the odds of a quarter-point rate hike in December at 76.8%, up from 66.9% a week earlier. Reuters reported that the data gives the Fed more room to focus on inflation tied to the conflict in the Middle East.

A note on sources: some summaries of Friday's jobs data described the December odds as a rate cut. The CME FedWatch figures reported by Fox Business, CNBC and Reuters coverage refer to a rate hike, so that is what we use here.
Our take (opinion)
Timmer's numbers are a snapshot, not a law of nature. Correlations shift. In a sharp sell-off, investors tend to sell whatever they can, including the things they normally treat as safe. That says something about this month. It doesn't settle what bitcoin or gold will do over the next several years.
The useful lesson is to separate price from ownership. Nobody controls the price of bitcoin on any given Friday, not even Fidelity. What you control is where your coins are kept and who holds the keys. The case for owning your own money, without asking a bank or a platform for permission, doesn't depend on a good week for stocks.
