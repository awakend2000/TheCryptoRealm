In a recent interview clip, a guest identified as Alex says digital ID is "one of the critical pieces" of a digital control grid. It is a strong claim, and a lot of people share the worry behind it. So it is worth separating what is documented, what has changed since the interview, and what is still a prediction.
The biggest update first. The interviewer talks about Keir Starmer being in power. Since then, the UK's national digital ID plan has been cancelled.

What the guest argued
Digital ID is one piece of a bigger system. He says it is paired with digital currency, the tokenization of assets, and what he describes as a universal blockchain ledger being built by the Bank for International Settlements (BIS).
It would create a permanent record of what you do. He says it would not stay a card you show at the airport. You would need it for the internet, health care, banking and purchases, and AI could then build a detailed profile of each person.
It could be tied to compliance. He compares it to China's social credit system, which he calls a pilot for the world, and says access to services could depend on behavior. He points back to the COVID-era restrictions as a preview.
You could not opt out. He says that once you take it, there is no way off, and he predicts "the end of human freedom."
He is giving his opinion and his predictions, not reporting that these steps have been taken.
What has changed in the UK
Starmer announced the plan in late September 2025, saying digital ID would be mandatory to prove the right to work by the end of the Parliament. The backlash was fast. A petition against digital ID cards passed 2.9 million signatures by Oct. 23, 2025, one of the largest in the history of Parliament's petitions site.

In December 2025 the House of Commons debated the petition, and all opposition parties and many Labour MPs criticized the plan, according to the House of Commons Library. In January 2026 the government dropped the mandatory element. In July 2026, incoming Prime Minister Andy Burnham announced he would scrap the scheme altogether. A Commons Library briefing from September 2026 says the plans have been cancelled.

It is not a total win for privacy campaigners. Al Jazeera reported that the decision does not end the government's wider digital transformation programme, and that one campaigner warned other online government systems could grow into a de facto digital ID. The Conservatives questioned whether it was a real reversal. And Big Brother Watch says social media age restrictions could lead to a mandatory ID for the internet by another route.
Britain has been here before. Burnham worked on Tony Blair's ID card scheme as a Home Office minister, and that scheme was scrapped in 2010, according to Al Jazeera. The Tony Blair Institute was among the think tanks that proposed a national digital ID, the Commons Library notes.
What is documented elsewhere
The EU is rolling out a digital ID wallet, but it is voluntary. Under a 2024 regulation, all 27 EU member states must offer at least one digital identity wallet by Dec. 24, 2026. Law firm briefings say using it is voluntary, public services must accept it, and regulated businesses such as banks must accept it a year later. Euronews notes that the law bars governments from tracking wallet use, but the app still has to check with government servers that credentials are valid.
The BIS ledger is real, but it is a bank-to-bank project. The BIS's Project Agorá is testing a "unified ledger" with seven central banks and more than 40 financial firms. In July 2026, 28 institutions completed test transactions worth about 800,000 Swiss francs. The BIS describes it as a platform for wholesale cross-border payments between banks. It says it is not meant to replace correspondent banking. The published descriptions we reviewed focus on bank settlements, not on individuals' accounts or identity.
China has blacklists, but no single score. Researchers at the Mercator Institute for China Studies (MERICS) say a national social credit "score" is a myth. What exists is a fragmented set of blacklists and records that mostly targets businesses, though people with unpaid court judgments can be restricted, for example from buying some travel tickets. That is still a form of control, but it is not the single rating system many people imagine.
The other side
Supporters say digital ID can make public services easier to use and cut fraud and illegal working. That was the UK government's case. Designers of the EU wallet say it is built so you can prove one fact, such as your age, without handing over everything else. Critics reply that systems built for convenience tend to expand, and that trust in how governments handle data is low. Big Brother Watch cited YouGov polling in which 63% of Britons did not trust the government to keep their data secure.
My take (opinion)
The guest's biggest claim is that once you take digital ID there is no way off. What happened in the UK points the other way: 2.9 million people said no, and the plan was dropped. Public pressure works. In the EU the wallet is voluntary by law, so the thing to watch is whether it stays that way.
The principle I care about is simple. Identity systems, like money, should be optional and limited. That is a big reason I believe in Bitcoin. If you hold your own keys in a self-custody wallet, nobody asks who you are before you can hold or send your own money. Two honest caveats: regulated exchanges do require you to verify your identity when you buy, and Bitcoin transactions are public on the blockchain, so it is not the same as anonymous.
